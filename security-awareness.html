Lesson 1 · AWR-01

Spotting a Phishing Email

Phishing is a message that pretends to come from someone you trust so that you click a link, open an attachment, send money, or hand over your password. It targets people, not software, so no firewall stops it.

How it works

Most phishing combines a few recognisable moves. Check the actual sender address, not just the display name. Hover over links before clicking to see the real destination. Be suspicious of urgency (“within 24 hours”) and of any request for your password, which a real IT desk never needs. 

From: "University IT Desk" <support@univ-helpdesk.co>      ← not the university domain
Subject: Your account closes in 24 hours                 ← urgency
Link text: portal.university.edu
Real link: http://portal-university.verify-login.co       ← look-alike domain

How it is prevented

  • ✓Verify unexpected requests through a channel you already trust, such as the official website or a known phone number.
  • ✓Report suspicious messages to IT instead of deleting them, so others are warned.
  • ✓Turn on multi-factor authentication, so a stolen password alone is not enough.

Real-world case

“Business email compromise”, 2013–2015

A Lithuanian man posed as a real Asian hardware supplier and emailed fake invoices to two US technology companies, tricking them into wiring over US$100 million. He pleaded guilty to wire fraud in 2019.

Source: US Department of Justice (2019)

Quick check

An email shows the sender as "PSU IT Desk". Does that prove it came from PSU?

Show answer

No. The display name can be anything; check the actual address after it, and verify through a channel you trust.

Lesson 2 · AWR-02

Passwords That Survive an Attack

Attackers rarely guess passwords one by one by hand. They run wordlist attacks: millions of real passwords from earlier breaches, plus common variations, tried automatically.

How it works

Swapping letters for look-alike symbols (P@ssw0rd) or adding a year and an exclamation mark (Summer2026!) are the first variations cracking tools try. What makes a password hard to crack is mostly length and unpredictability: a passphrase of several random words beats a short “complex” password. 

P@ssw0rd123                          ← in every wordlist, with variations
correct horse battery staple river   ← long, random words: far harder to guess

How it is prevented

  • ✓Use long passphrases, and a password manager so every account gets a different one.
  • ✓Turn on multi-factor authentication wherever it is offered.
  • ✓NIST guidance asks services to allow long passwords, check new passwords against lists of breached ones, and drop arbitrary composition rules.

Real-world case

RockYou, 2009

A breach of the social-app company RockYou exposed more than 32 million user accounts, whose passwords had been stored in plain text. Leaked lists like this one are exactly what wordlist attacks are built from.

Source: TechCrunch (2009)

Quick check

Which is stronger: "Tr0ub4dor&3" or "orange piano sunset ladder"?

Show answer

Usually the four-word passphrase: it is longer and its words are random, while symbol swaps are predictable to cracking tools.

Lesson 3 · AWR-03

Social Engineering by Phone

Social engineering manipulates people into giving away access. On the phone (vishing), an attacker invents a believable pretext, such as a new IT contractor, an auditor, or a stressed manager, and relies on politeness and urgency.

How it works

A skilled caller rarely asks for a password. They collect small, harmless-sounding facts (your manager’s name, your date of birth, your first pet, which systems you use) and combine them to pass account-recovery questions or to sound convincing on the next call. 

"Hi, I'm with the IT contractor doing the email migration. Just confirming
 your details: you report to Ms. Al-Harbi, right? And your date of birth
 for the record?"    ← each answer sounds harmless; together they reset an account

How it is prevented

  • ✓Verify callers by calling back on a number you look up yourself, never one they give you.
  • ✓Treat personal details as security information: they are often account-recovery answers.
  • ✓Help desks need a fixed verification procedure that does not bend for urgency.

Real-world case

Twitter, July 2020

Attackers phoned Twitter employees while posing as the company’s IT staff and talked some of them into entering their credentials. They took over high-profile accounts and used them to run a scam that collected more than US$118,000 in bitcoin, according to the New York Department of Financial Services.

Source: NYDFS Twitter investigation report (2020)

Quick check

A caller never asks for your password. Can the call still be an attack?

Show answer

Yes. Harmless-looking details such as your birth date or manager’s name are often enough to reset an account or impersonate you.

What You Take Away

Awareness is a habit rather than a tool, and it is the control that scales to every employee.

  • Verify through another channel

    If a request is unexpected, confirm it using contact details you already had, never the ones in the message.

  • Urgency is the warning sign

    Pressure to act immediately is the most reliable indicator of a social engineering attempt.

  • Use a manager and MFA

    Unique long passwords plus a second factor mean one stolen credential does not open everything.

  • Report, do not just delete

    Reporting a phishing attempt protects colleagues who received the same message.
